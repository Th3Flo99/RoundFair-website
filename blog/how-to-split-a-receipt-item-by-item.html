How to Split a Receipt Item by Item

2026-09-19 · By FairLead

Assign every line on the receipt to a person, split shared dishes among the people who had them, then add tax and tip in proportion to each person's subtotal. Check that all shares add up to the final total.

Itemising is the most exact way to split a bill, and the one most people avoid because it feels like homework. It does not have to. With a clear sequence and one worked example, a six-person receipt takes about five minutes. This guide gives the steps, works through an example including shared plates, explains how to handle awkward items like shared bottles and discounts, and shows when itemising is worth the effort.

Step by step

  1. Photograph the receipt so everyone can see it.
  2. Assign each item to one person, or several for shared plates.
  3. Divide shared items equally among those who shared.
  4. Total each person's items to get their subtotal.
  5. Add tax and tip in proportion. The formula is on our tax and tip page.
  6. Check that the shares add up to the final total.

Worked example

Two people share nachos (€8) and each has a main: Lea €14, Tom €18. Lea's subtotal is 4 + 14 = €18; Tom's is 4 + 18 = €22. Bill subtotal is €40. With a final total of €46, Lea pays €20.70 and Tom pays €25.30.

LeaHalf nachos (€4) + main (€14)€18€20.70
TomHalf nachos (€4) + main (€18)€22€25.30

The check: €20.70 + €25.30 = €46.00. If your shares do not add up to the final total, find the rounding difference and put it on one person.

A bigger example with drinks

Four friends. Food subtotal €60, including a shared platter of €16 (four ways). Drinks: Ana two cocktails (€22), Ben two beers (€12), Cleo water (€3), Dan a beer and a cocktail (€17). Total food and drink €114 before tip. With a 10% tip the final total is €125.40.

Each person's subtotal is their own food plus a quarter of the platter (€4) plus their drinks. Multiply each subtotal by 125.40 ÷ 114 (1.10) to get the final share. The tip lands in proportion, so Cleo, who had the water, does not pay a quarter of it.

Handle the awkward bits

  • Shared bottle: divide among drinkers only. See when some friends don't drink.
  • Discounts: apply them to the subtotal before calculating shares.
  • Rounding: round up and let one person absorb the cent.
  • Service charge: treat it like tip, in proportion to each subtotal.
  • Forgotten items: if the receipt has items nobody claims, split them evenly rather than arguing.

Save time with a template

Write each name on a line with three columns: own items, shared items, total. Fill the first column from the photo, the second by dividing shared plates, then add. A phone note is enough. If you do this often, a spreadsheet with one formula for tax and tip saves the last step.

Split a receipt at the table in five minutes

  • Photograph the receipt the moment it arrives, before anyone marks it.
  • Read the items aloud and let each person claim theirs. Unclaimed items go to the group.
  • Use a calculator for shares of shared plates, then add up each person.
  • Apply tax and tip as one multiplier: final total divided by bill subtotal.
  • Confirm the shares add up before anyone pays.

The multiplier step is the shortcut that makes itemising quick. One division gives a single number to multiply every subtotal by, so you never have to calculate tax and tip person by person.

A complete worked example with tax, tip and a discount

Four friends dine. The receipt shows: starters shared €18, Ana's main €24, Ben's main €20, Cleo's main €22, Dan's main €26, drinks Ana €12, Ben €8, Cleo €4, Dan €10, and a €10 voucher discount. The subtotal is €18 + €92 + €34 = €144 before the voucher, and €134 after it. Tax is included in the prices. A 10 per cent tip is added to the discounted subtotal, so the final total is €147.40.

Step by step: the voucher reduces the subtotal to €134, so each person's share is reduced in proportion. Shared starters are €4.50 each. Personal subtotals: Ana €24 + €12 + €4.50 = €40.50, Ben €20 + €8 + €4.50 = €32.50, Cleo €22 + €4 + €4.50 = €30.50, Dan €26 + €10 + €4.50 = €40.50. These add to €144. The multiplier is 147.40 ÷ 144 = 1.0236. Ana pays €41.46, Ben €33.27, Cleo €31.22, Dan €41.46, totalling about €147.41, so one person absorbs a cent.

Splitting items when people share half portions

Sometimes two people share one main, or one person has a bite of another's dessert. Do not over-engineer it. If two people shared a dish, split its cost between them. If someone tasted a dish, ignore it. The goal is fairness within a reasonable tolerance, not accounting perfection. A rule that works is: split any item that more than half of the table shared, and ignore tastes and sips. Agree this at the start, and the arguments never begin.

Fill in one row per person, apply the multiplier to the subtotal, and check that the last column adds up to the bill. It fits on a phone screen, and it scales to any size of group.

When the receipt is a mess

Some receipts are hard to read: faded ink, abbreviations, items grouped by course. Ask the server for an itemised bill, or a printed version. If the restaurant uses a table app, it may show a list of items you can claim. If you have already paid a combined bill, take a photo of the list and go through it line by line while memory is fresh. And if you cannot identify an item, split it evenly and move on. A few cents of disagreement is not worth a long discussion.

Tools for itemised splitting

You can itemise with nothing more than a photo and a calculator, but a few tools help. A spreadsheet with one row per item and one column per person makes it easy to see who had what and to spot mistakes. A notes app works for small groups. Some apps let you assign items to people and calculate tax and tip. Check each app's own page, since features vary, and remember that RoundFair does not scan receipts, and is built for rounds and simple bill splits instead.

Whatever you use, the method is the same: assign, total, multiply and check. Tools speed it up, but they do not change the logic. If you understand the steps, you can do it on a napkin.

Fairness questions that come up

Should the person who chose the expensive restaurant pay more?

Not usually. If the group agreed to the venue, everyone pays for what they ate. If someone was persuaded against their budget, it is kind to let them order lightly without comment.

What about the person who ate the most of a shared dish?

Do not police it. Split shared dishes evenly among those who shared. Tracking bites causes far more friction than it saves money.

What if someone ordered something and did not eat it?

They still pay, unless they returned it. A dish that arrives wrong is a matter for the restaurant.

Should birthday people pay?

Usually not. Agree in advance, and split their share among the others.

Itemising a bar tab

A bar receipt is often long and repetitive: six lines for the same beer, three for the same cocktail. Itemising is possible, but it is slow, and it is easy to lose track of who ordered what. The alternative is to log each round as it is bought. Each round is recorded with its cost, and the consumption overview shows who had what. That is essentially itemising in advance, one round at a time, which is easier than reconstructing it from a receipt at midnight. See round buying vs splitting the bill.

Save the receipt, save the argument

Keep a photo of the receipt until everything is settled. If a question arises later, such as whether a dish was included or how the tip was calculated, the photo answers it. Delete it afterwards if you like, but do not throw away the paper until money has changed hands. A surprising number of disputes come from a receipt that was thrown away too soon. Store it in a shared album or send it to the group chat, so everyone can check the figures.

Five rules of thumb for itemising

  1. Agree to itemise before you order. Otherwise it feels like an accusation.
  2. Split shared items among those who shared them. No policing bites.
  3. Share tax, tip and service in proportion. Use the multiplier.
  4. Round at the end, not in the middle. Intermediate rounding compounds errors.
  5. Check the total. If it does not match, find the missing item.

These five rules cover nearly every bill, whether it has five items or fifty.

Itemising for a mixed table of drinkers and non-drinkers

Itemising shines when a table mixes drinkers and non-drinkers, because each person pays for what they consumed. The steps are the same: assign each item, split shared plates, add personal drinks, then apply tax and tip in proportion. The non-drinker's subtotal is smaller, so their share of the tip is smaller too. See splitting a bill when some friends do not drink for a full example and the etiquette around it. And if the evening involves many rounds, tracking each round as it is bought is easier than itemising a long receipt at the end.

A short FAQ on itemising etiquette

Should I itemise if only one person had a lot more? Ask them to add a little on top, and keep the rest equal. Should I itemise at a first meeting? Usually no: split evenly or pay for your own. What if someone is embarrassed about their order? Do not comment. Everyone eats differently. Should I mention a mistake in the bill? Yes, politely, and check with the server before adding it to the split. What if the total is off by a few cents? Adjust one share and move on. Itemising works best when it is treated as a routine task and not a judgement about anyone's choices.

How do I split a receipt when shared items are not shared equally?

Equal division of shared plates is the default, but it is not always fair. Imagine five people order a large pizza (€15) and one of them eats only a single slice out of eight. Splitting it five ways charges the light eater €3 for a slice worth under €2. The fix is to split by portions instead of by heads: count the slices, divide the price by eight (€1.875 a slice), and charge each person for what they took. Two people who ate three slices each pay €5.625; the single-slice person pays €1.875; and so on.

Portions are more work, so use them only when the imbalance is large. A good rule: if the shared item is under about 10% of the bill, split it equally and move on. If it is a large share of the bill, such as a seafood tower or a bottle of wine, agree the split before anyone pays.

What does a full example with tax, tip and a discount look like?

Three friends, one receipt. Item prices: Mia has a salad (€12) and a glass of wine (€6); Sam has a burger (€16) and a beer (€5); Joy has pasta (€15) and sparkling water (€3). They also split a dessert (€9) three ways, so each carries €3. The restaurant applies a 10% voucher to the food and drink subtotal, adds 8% tax and the group leaves a 10% tip on the discounted subtotal.

  1. Subtotals: Mia 12 + 6 + 3 = €21. Sam 16 + 5 + 3 = €24. Joy 15 + 3 + 3 = €21. Bill subtotal €66.
  2. Discount: 10% off leaves €59.40, so the multiplier for the discount is 0.90.
  3. Tax and tip: 8% tax and 10% tip on €59.40 add €4.75 and €5.94, so the final total is €70.09.
  4. Multiplier: 70.09 ÷ 66 = 1.0620.
  5. Shares: Mia 21 × 1.062 = €22.30, Sam 24 × 1.062 = €25.49, Joy €22.30. Together: €70.09.

Notice that the discount, tax and tip are all folded into one multiplier. That is the trick from our tax and tip guide: work out the final total once, divide by the bill subtotal, and apply the result to every person.

What are the most common mistakes when itemising a bill?

  • Adding tip on top of a service charge. Check the receipt first. Many places already include a charge, and a second tip doubles it.
  • Forgetting the shared items. A bowl of chips ordered for the table is easy to miss when everyone claims only their own plate. Tick every line as it is assigned.
  • Applying tax to each person separately and rounding each time. Rounding five times creates cent errors. Use the multiplier, round once at the end and let one person absorb the difference.
  • Splitting a discount evenly. A voucher on food only should not reduce the drinks-only person's share. Apply it to the lines it covers.
  • Assigning rounds of drinks to the wrong person. If someone bought a round, the drinks belong to whoever drank them, not whoever paid.

Who should pay first, and how do we settle afterwards?

Most tables let one person pay the whole bill on a card and collect from the others. Pick the person who is most likely to get points or who will not mind waiting, and hand them a screenshot or note of everyone's share. Ask the others to pay back the same day, while the evening is still fresh. If you regularly go out with the same group, see how to remind friends to pay you back for polite wording, and how to split and charge friends on Venmo if a payment app is easier than cash.

What if the waiter is waiting and nobody has time to itemise?

Pay first and calculate later. Photograph the receipt, pay with one card and send the itemised shares afterwards. The photo is the record: it shows each line, so nobody needs to remember what they ordered. A compromise that works well is to itemise only the big-ticket differences (bottles, steaks, cocktails) and split everything else equally. That gets you 90% of the fairness for 20% of the effort, and it is a good fit with the approaches listed on our dinner with friends page.

Frequently asked questions

Can an app scan a receipt for me?

Some apps do. RoundFair does not scan receipts; it focuses on rounds and simple bill splits.

How do I split shared appetisers?

Divide by the number of people who ate them and add that to each of their subtotals.

What if the bill has a discount?

Subtract it from the subtotal first, then calculate proportional shares.

What do I do if the shares do not add up to the total?

Find the rounding difference and put it on one person, so the total is exact.

Is itemising worth it for small bills?

Usually not. If orders are similar or the sums are small, an equal split is quicker and fair enough.

Should I split tax and tip before or after itemising?

Itemise first to get each person's subtotal, then apply tax and tip as one multiplier: the final total divided by the bill subtotal.

What if someone ordered nothing but shared a bottle?

Charge them only their part of the bottle, plus their share of any tip in proportion, so they pay for what they actually had.
