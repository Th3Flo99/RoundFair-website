How to Split a Bill and Charge Friends on Venmo

2026-09-19 · By Florian Ronsse

Work out each person's share first, then open your payment app, choose Request, enter the amount and add a clear note such as "Dinner at Luigi's". Venmo, PayPal, Apple Cash, Revolut and Tikkie all work this way.

Payment apps are the last step of splitting a bill, not the first. They move money, but they leave the decision about who owes what to you. This guide walks through the process from working out the shares to sending the request, compares the main payment apps, explains how to get paid faster, and shows what to do when a night of rounds means nobody needs to send anything.

Step 1: Work out who owes what

Payment apps move money but do not decide the split. Do the maths first: divide the final total including tax and tip, or use percentages when orders differ. Not sure which method to use? See bill split methods explained.

Write the amounts down before you open the app. It is much easier to type three numbers you already know than to calculate while the friend watches you fumble.

Step 2: Request the money

  1. Open the app and tap Pay or Request.
  2. Choose the friend and enter their exact amount.
  3. Add a note with the place and date.
  4. Choose Request, not Pay.

The last step is the one people get wrong. Tapping Pay instead of Request sends your money, which is an easy mistake at the end of a long evening. Take one second to check the button before you confirm.

Write a note that gets paid

The note is the most underrated part of a request. A note like "Dinner at Luigi's, 12 May" tells the friend exactly what it is for, which removes the excuse of not remembering. Add the split if it is not obvious: "€28 each, 6 people". Avoid jokes that could be mistaken for a real dispute, and avoid vague notes like "stuff", which invite questions.

Tips for getting paid faster

  • Request the same evening, while it is fresh.
  • Use one message with the total and the per-person amount.
  • Round to a clean number rather than chasing cents.
  • Request from everyone at once, so nobody feels singled out.
  • If someone forgets, send a friendly nudge. See how to remind friends to pay you back.

Common mistakes

  • Paying instead of requesting. Check the button.
  • Forgetting the tip. Work out the total including tip first. See how to split tax and tip.
  • Requesting the wrong amount. Double-check the digits before you send.
  • Waiting a week. The longer you wait, the more likely the friend has forgotten what it was.

What to do if you are the one who owes

The same rules work in reverse. When you receive a request, pay it the same day if you can, and reply with a short thanks. If the amount looks wrong, ask politely and give the number you expected: "I think it was €24 each, not €28. Should we check the receipt?" Most mistakes are genuine, and a calm message solves them quickly.

If you cannot pay right away, say when you can. "Sending Friday" is much better than silence, and it saves the other person from chasing. Being a good payer is the easiest way to be a friend who never needs reminding.

Splitting a bill unevenly in a payment app

Payment apps let you request a different amount from each person, which is exactly what an uneven split needs. The workflow is: calculate each share, then create one request per person with their own amount and a shared note. Some apps offer a split tool that divides a total evenly, but if you need an uneven split, you enter each amount manually.

Give each request the same note so the group can see it is one event: "Luigi's, 12 May". If you need to include a tip, calculate it first, so the requested amount is the final amount. And if you fronted the bill, remember to include your own share in the maths, so the total requested equals the bill minus what you owe yourself.

Privacy and etiquette in payment apps

Some payment apps show transactions to a wider audience by default. Before you send a request, check your privacy settings, and avoid notes that reveal more than you intend. A note like "Luigi's, 12 May" is fine; a note that discloses a friend's financial situation or a personal joke may not be. Emoji-only notes are common but easy to misread, so use words when the amount is large.

Etiquette-wise, send requests promptly, keep the tone neutral and do not use the note field to nag. If you need to remind someone, send a message instead. Public shaming through payment notes may feel funny to you and land badly with them. See how to remind friends to pay you back for wording that works.

Fees, limits and delays

Payment apps differ in their fees and limits, and these change often, so check the app's own page. As a general guide, sending money from a bank balance is often free, while using a credit card or an instant transfer may cost a small percentage. Some apps limit how much you can send in a period, and transfers to a bank account can take a day or more. If you are settling a large amount, such as a group trip, check the limits before the day and consider a bank transfer instead.

Also think about currency. If friends live in different countries, the apps available to them may differ, and a bank transfer or a widely available service may be simpler. See how to split expenses in a foreign currency.

When you fronted the whole trip or tab

If you paid a large bill for the group, it is worth being organised. Write down the total and each person's share. Send all requests at once, with a clear deadline. Keep a note of who has paid, and follow up once. If the amounts are large, a bank transfer with a reference is safer and leaves a clear record. And be gracious: people are more prompt when the request is friendly and easy to act on.

For nights out, avoid the problem in the first place by tracking rounds as you go. RoundFair shows who is behind, and Split the bill works out the single transfer that settles the night, so there is nothing large to chase afterwards.

Requests versus payment links

Some apps let you send a request to a specific person, while others generate a payment link that anyone can open. Requests are personal and tracked: you see who has paid. Links are flexible: you can post one in a group chat, and anyone can pay it. For a group of friends, a request per person is neater. For a larger group or a gift collection, a link is simpler. Choose the one that fits the group, and be consistent, so nobody wonders which to use.

A checklist for organisers

  1. Work out the total and each share.
  2. Decide the deadline, and say it.
  3. Send requests the same evening.
  4. Include a clear note.
  5. Record who has paid.
  6. Follow up once after a few days.
  7. Say thank you when it arrives.

This list works for a dinner, a trip or a night of rounds. The steps are the same: be clear, be prompt, be kind.

Rounds and payment apps: the light approach

A night out with rounds does not need a request per drink. Take turns buying, track the totals, and send one payment at the end if it is needed. That is the approach RoundFair supports: log each round, see who is behind and use Split the bill to work out the difference. A single small transfer replaces a string of requests and reminders. A worked example: a birthday dinner for seven

Seven friends celebrate a birthday. The final bill is €266 including tip. The birthday person, Ana, does not pay, so the other six split €266, which is €44.33 each. Round up to €45, which raises €270 and leaves €4 extra for a small gift or to cover any difference. The organiser, Ben, paid the card, so he requests €45 from five friends and keeps his own share.

The note for every request reads "Ana's birthday, Luigi's, 12 May". The requests go out the same evening. By the next morning, four have paid, and one sends a reminder-worthy silence. A single friendly nudge, "Quick reminder for Ana's dinner, €45, no rush", settles it. The whole process takes ten minutes of effort and one tap per person, and nobody is left out of pocket.

What if a friend does not use the same app?

Not everyone has the same payment app, especially across countries. Have a fallback ready. A bank transfer with your details is universal, and many banks support instant transfers. Some apps let you send a payment link that works even if the recipient does not have the app. If your friend is in a different country, agree the currency and any fees in advance. Never insist on an app; the aim is to get paid, not to convert a friend. A simple message with your bank details and the reference works in nearly every case, and it avoids the friction that makes people put a request off.

Finally, keep records. A screenshot or a note of who has paid, kept until everyone has, saves confusion later.

Security and common-sense precautions

Payment apps involve real money, so a few precautions are wise. Only send or request money with people you know. Double-check the recipient's name before you confirm. Be cautious of unexpected requests, even from contacts, since accounts can be compromised. Use a passcode or biometric lock on the app. Keep your notes free of sensitive information. And if something looks wrong, contact the app's support at once. None of this is complicated, but a moment of caution can prevent a costly mistake. Once you have a routine, splitting bills through an app is both quick and safe.

How do I request different amounts from several friends at once?

When orders differ, one flat request will not do. Work out each person's share first, then send separate requests, one per person, each with the same note. A dinner for five where the total is €150 might look like this: Ana €38 (she had the steak), Ben €27, Cleo €27, Dan €30 and you, having paid, covering your own €28. Four requests, four different amounts, one shared note: "Luigi's, 12 May, thanks!"

Check that the requested amounts plus your own share equal the receipt total: 38 + 27 + 27 + 30 + 28 = €150. If they do not, you have either forgotten someone or mis-typed a digit. It takes ten seconds and prevents the awkward "I think you asked for too much" message later. For unequal orders, the itemised method in how to split a receipt item by item gives you each figure.

What is the best way to round the amounts?

Chasing cents helps nobody. A bill of €143.60 between five people is €28.72 each, which looks fussy on a request. Round to €29 and the payer collects €145, a €1.40 cushion. Whether that is fair depends on your group. Some friends round up and let the payer keep the change as thanks for fronting the bill; others prefer exact amounts. Decide once, say so in the group chat, and stick to it.

A useful rule: round each person's share up to the nearest euro or dollar when the bill is over about 100, and keep exact cents on smaller bills. If the payer ends up ahead by more than a few euros, either return the difference or roll it into next time. Never round in your own favour silently, because that is the kind of thing people remember.

What if a friend does not use the same payment app?

Not everyone has Venmo, and it is mainly used in the United States. A friend abroad might prefer a bank transfer, PayPal or Revolut. Ask before the evening ends, not after: "What's easiest for you to pay?" Then give them the amount and details in one message. The note stays the same, and the amount is what matters. Keep a short list of who prefers what for groups you meet regularly, because the same person will almost always pick the same method.

  • Bank transfer: send the amount and a reference such as "Luigi's 12 May".
  • Cash: agree it at the table, while the wallets are already out.
  • Payment link: some apps, such as Tikkie in the Netherlands, let you send a link instead of a request.

What does a complete example look like, from receipt to payment?

Six friends eat out. The receipt shows €168 plus a 10% tip, so the final total is €184.80. Here is the sequence a sensible organiser would follow.

  1. At the table: photograph the receipt and pay with your own card so the group can leave promptly.
  2. On the way home: calculate €184.80 ÷ 6 = €30.80 each, and round to €31, a total of €186.
  3. Before you sleep: open your payment app and send five requests of €31 with the note "Luigi's, 12 May".
  4. Next morning: check who has paid. Tick each name in a note.
  5. Day three: nudge anyone still outstanding, with one friendly line.

The €1.20 over-collected can go toward next time or be given back. What matters is that the work is done in the first evening. The longer you wait, the more likely it is that the request feels like a new demand rather than part of the night out.

Frequently asked questions

Can Venmo split a bill automatically?

Venmo lets you request different amounts from several people, but you calculate the split yourself.

What should I write in the note?

The place and date, for example 'Dinner at Luigi's, 12 May'. Clear notes get paid faster.

Is it rude to request money from friends?

No, as long as you are prompt and friendly. A clear request is easier than an awkward memory.

What if I tap Pay instead of Request?

You will have sent your own money. Check the button before confirming, and ask the friend to send it back if it happens.

Do I need a payment app for a night of rounds?

Not usually. If you take turns and track the rounds, one small transfer at the end settles any difference.

Should I round the amounts I request?

Rounding up to a clean number is fine if the group agrees. Decide the rule once and tell everyone, so nobody feels the payer is quietly profiting.

What if a friend does not use Venmo?

Ask which method suits them, such as a bank transfer, PayPal or cash, and send the same amount and note by that route.
