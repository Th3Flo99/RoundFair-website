Round Buying App: Track Whose Turn It Is

2026-09-19 · By FairLead

If you have ever lost track of who bought the last round, an app can end the argument for good. A good round buying app records each round as it happens, shows whose turn is next, and works out the final split when the night is over, so nobody has to rely on memory or a note on their phone.

This article explains what a round buying app should actually do, the features that sound useful but usually are not, and how RoundFair handles the whole evening from the first drink to the last settlement.

Why use an app for rounds?

Round buying is one of the oldest social systems in the world, and it has one weakness: it depends on everyone's memory. Add a few drinks and a loud room and the memory fails. A phone does not.

An app also solves the deeper problem, which is that most groups track turns when they should track money. Whose turn it is tells you very little if one round was £18 and the next £62. An app that records amounts can show who is genuinely behind rather than who has bought the fewest times. See round buying vs splitting the bill for why the two approaches give different answers.

What a round buying app should do

  • Log a round in seconds. If it takes more than a few taps, people will stop using it by round three.
  • Show whose turn it is. A clear "up now" indicator that everyone can see ends the "I think it's Sam's turn" debate.
  • Track who is behind. Turns are not enough; the app should track what each person has paid and highlight the person who owes the most.
  • Handle a random pick. A built-in spin wheel or picker saves you from coin tosses and rigged claims.
  • Split the bill at the end. When you decide to settle, it should calculate who owes whom.
  • Work without a signal. Pubs and bars often have terrible reception, so offline support is essential.
  • Be free of friction. No sign-up wall, no forcing every friend to install it.

What to skip

Some features look impressive in a screenshot but slow down a real evening:

  • Detailed itemising of every drink. Tracking each pint separately is tedious. Track the round total unless you need the detail.
  • Mandatory accounts for everyone. If every friend has to register before the first drink, the app never gets used.
  • Social feeds and badges. A leaderboard of who buys most is a novelty, not a solution.
  • Ads that interrupt the moment. A tool for the bar table should stay out of the way.

How a round tracker compares with the alternatives

Before installing anything, it is worth being honest about the alternatives, because many groups already have a system that half works.

Memory

Free, instant and unreliable. It works for two or three people over an hour, and it fails as soon as the group grows, the night lengthens or the drinks vary in price. It also favours whoever argues loudest.

A notes app or group chat

Better than memory, since it survives the night. The drawback is friction: someone has to type, format and add up. By the third round the note is out of date, and by the end nobody trusts it.

A spreadsheet

Accurate if maintained, but nobody wants to open a spreadsheet at a bar. It is the right tool for a weekend trip and the wrong one for a Friday.

A general bill-splitting or ledger app

These are excellent for households and holidays, where many people pay for many things over days. On a night out, they ask you to log every purchase and assign every share, which is more work than the evening deserves. See the best bill splitting apps and RoundFair vs Splitwise.

A round tracker

Built for the one question that matters on the night: whose turn is it? It logs a round in a few taps, shows who is up next, and leaves the heavy accounting for the end.

Setting up your first session

The first session is the one that decides whether the app becomes a habit, so it helps to keep it simple.

  1. Create the session at the start of the night. Do it while you are waiting for the first drinks, when nobody is busy.
  2. Add the people who are there. You can add latecomers later, so do not wait for everyone.
  3. Log the first round straight away. The habit forms in the first ten minutes or not at all.
  4. Leave the phone on the table. A visible screen gives everyone the same information, which is what settles arguments.
  5. Settle at the end. When the group is ready to leave, use Split the bill and send any transfers before you go.

Because RoundFair is offline-first, none of this needs a signal, and because it needs no account, you can start before your friends have finished their first drink.

Fair play: how an app helps quiet friends

Round buying has a social cost that is easy to overlook. In every group there is somebody who would rather pay more than raise an awkward subject. They do not complain when they buy two rounds to everyone else's one; they just quietly pay. Over months, that adds up to resentment or a friendship that feels one-sided.

A visible record helps precisely those people. When the screen says it is Alex's turn, nobody has to say it, and the quiet friend does not have to be the one to bring it up. It turns a personal request into a neutral fact. That is a small but real benefit of using an app, and it has nothing to do with technology and everything to do with making it easy to be fair.

Common questions about using an app at the bar

People who have never used a tracker tend to have the same worries, and they are all fair. Will it kill the mood? Logging a round takes a few seconds and happens once per round, which is less disruption than the argument it replaces. Will friends mind? Most people are relieved that someone else is keeping track, since it removes the need to remember. What if my phone dies? Anyone at the table can hold the session, and because nothing depends on a live connection, it keeps working offline.

The most useful tip is to appoint a keeper for the night. Whoever is sitting closest to the middle of the table logs the round, and the job can rotate. It should never be the same person every time, or it becomes a chore rather than a shared habit. Keep the screen visible so the group can see it, and the whole system becomes self-policing in a friendly way.

Who a round tracker is really for

Some groups will get far more value from a tracker than others. It suits friends who go out regularly, since the habit compounds and the group stops thinking about fairness at all. It suits mixed groups of drinkers and non-drinkers, because amounts are recorded and nobody has to explain. It suits big groups, where memory fails first. And it suits anyone who has ever quietly paid more than their share and wished someone else would bring it up. It is less useful for a one-off drink for two, where a coin toss is enough.

Getting the most out of a round tracker

A few habits make a tracker work better. Log the round as soon as the drinks arrive, while the amount is fresh. Add latecomers as they show up so they are counted from that point. Keep the phone on the table where everyone can see whose turn it is, because a visible answer ends debates. Use the spin wheel for ties or for fun, and let the group know it is there. At the end of the night, settle with Split the bill before people head home, since transfers are easiest while everyone is still together. Finally, rotate the job of keeping the record, so it never feels like one person's chore. These small routines turn a useful tool into a comfortable habit, and after two or three nights most groups do them without thinking.

How do I set up a round tracking session in a busy pub?

Speed is everything, because nobody wants to stare at a phone while the group waits. The routine that works best is short.

  1. Nominate one person as the scorekeeper. It is usually whoever suggested the app. Others can ignore it.
  2. Add names, not details. First names are enough. Add latecomers when they arrive.
  3. Log each round as it lands on the table. Doing it while the buyer is still there means the amount is fresh.
  4. Glance at Up now before each round. Announce the name. That is the entire ritual.
  5. Settle at closing. Open Split the bill and let everyone see the result on one screen.

The turn order feature covers the second half of this in detail, and the pub rounds use case shows the flow for a regular Friday crowd.

What happens if someone forgets to log a round?

It will happen, usually around round five. The fix is easy: add the missed round as soon as you notice, using the price you remember or the bank transaction. A rough amount is far better than none, because the tally exists to catch big gaps, not to be exact to the penny. If nobody remembers the cost, agree an estimate and move on. Consider the small error a cost of using a simple tool rather than a reason to drop it.

What mistakes make round tracking apps fail in practice?

  • Making everyone install it. One keeper is enough, and sharing is optional.
  • Logging too late. If you enter three rounds at 11 pm you will guess the numbers.
  • Tracking too much detail. Round totals are enough for most nights. Itemise only if the group truly needs it, for example with who drank what.
  • Ignoring the tally. The app only helps if someone glances at it before the next round.
