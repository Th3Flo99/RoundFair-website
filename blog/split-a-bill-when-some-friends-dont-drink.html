Splitting a Bill When Some Friends Don't Drink

2026-09-19 · By FairLead

A mixed table is normal now: someone is driving, someone is pregnant, someone is training, someone simply does not drink. Yet the traditional bill split, whether an even split or a round of drinks, quietly assumes everyone had alcohol. The result is that non-drinkers pay for other people's drinks and feel too awkward to say so.

This guide shows how to split a bill fairly when some friends do not drink: separating food from drinks, working through an example, handling tip and shared items, dealing with rounds, and making sure non-drinkers feel included rather than penalised.

Why an even split is unfair to non-drinkers

If six friends split a €180 bill evenly, everyone pays €30. But if four had cocktails and two had water, the drinkers might have spent €38 each while the non-drinkers spent €14. The non-drinkers pay €16 too much, and the drinkers get a quiet subsidy.

The non-drinker often stays silent because raising it feels petty. The fix is not to make people ask; it is to make the fair method the default. See how to split a bar bill fairly for the general methods.

Separate food from drinks

The simplest fair approach is to split the bill into two parts:

  • Food, shared by everyone. Split evenly among all diners, since everyone ate.
  • Drinks, paid individually. Each person pays for their own drinks, or drinkers share the alcohol among themselves.

This takes a minute more than an even split, and it is fair to everyone. It also avoids the greyer question of who "had" a shared bottle: only those who drank from it pay for it.

A worked example

Six friends have dinner. Food is €90 in total (shared). Drinks are €104: four friends had two cocktails each at €12 (€96), while two had two sparkling waters each at €2 (€4 each, €8 in total), which makes the €104.

PersonFood shareDrinksTotal
Drinker (x4)€15€24€39
Non-drinker (x2)€15€4€19

The total is 4 × €39 + 2 × €19 = €194, which matches the €90 food plus €104 drinks. An even split would have cost everyone €32.33, so each non-drinker would have overpaid by €13.33.

Handle the tip and shared items

  • Tip. Share it in proportion to what each person spent. A non-drinker's tip share is smaller because their bill is smaller. See how much to tip when splitting the bill.
  • Shared starters. Split evenly between everyone who ate them.
  • Bottles for the table. Split between those who drank from them, not the whole table.
  • Soft drinks and coffee. These count as drinks, and each person pays for their own.

When rounds are involved

Rounds are harder for non-drinkers. If every round includes everyone, non-drinkers end up buying rounds of alcohol they did not drink, or receiving cheap soft drinks in exchange for buying expensive rounds.

Three fair options:

  • Non-drinkers opt out of rounds and pay for their own drinks. Simple, but they can feel left out.
  • Non-drinkers join, but only pay for the value of their own drinks when it is their turn. The rest of the round is covered by the drinkers.
  • Track amounts and settle. Everyone buys rounds as usual; at the end, compare what each person paid with what they consumed.

The third is the fairest and least awkward. See round buying vs splitting the bill for why.

Keep non-drinkers included

Fairness is not just about the numbers. Non-drinkers should feel part of the evening, not the odd one out:

  • Agree the split before ordering, so nobody has to bring it up.
  • Do not make a big point of it; treat it as the standard method.
  • Include them in the ritual of the round if they want to be, and let them take part with soft drinks.
  • Do not assume anyone is missing out because they are not drinking.

Conversations that go smoothly

  • Non-drinker to the group: "I'm only having water tonight, so I'll pay for my own drinks and share the food." Said early, it lands as a fact rather than a complaint.
  • Organiser to the table: "Food's split evenly, drinks are separate." Said once at the start, it becomes the default for everyone.
  • Drinker to non-drinker: "Don't worry about the round, we've got it." Generosity offered by the drinkers is welcome, but it should never be expected.

Why people do not drink, and why it matters for the bill

Non-drinkers are not one group. Some are driving, some are pregnant, some are on medication, some are in recovery, some are training, some avoid alcohol for religion or health, and some simply do not like it. The reason is private, and it should stay private. The practical point is that the bill should not reveal it or require an explanation.

A fair system therefore works without questions. Nobody has to say why they are not drinking; they just pay for what they had. That makes the method good etiquette as well as good arithmetic. Groups that default to "pay for what you had" for drinks avoid putting anyone in the position of explaining, justifying or asking for a discount.

How to make the default fair

The easiest way to avoid awkwardness is to set the norm before anyone has to ask. A host or organiser can say when booking: "We'll split food evenly and everyone pays for their own drinks." Phrased as the default, it reads as a policy, not a favour. Non-drinkers are then never the ones raising a question.

When rounds are involved, a similar norm works: "Rounds are for whoever is drinking, and non-drinkers sort their own." Or use a tracked tally where everyone settles by consumption at the end, and nobody has to opt out. Whichever you choose, it should be stated before the first round, not adjusted mid-evening.

A second example: pub night with one non-drinker

Five friends go out. Four drink pints at £6 and one drinks lime and soda at £2.50. They buy five rounds. Each round costs 4 × £6 + £2.50 = £26.50. Five rounds cost £132.50.

Consumption: each pint drinker £30, the soda drinker £12.50. An even split gives £26.50 each, so the soda drinker would overpay by £14 while the pint drinkers underpay by £3.50 each. Buying by rotation makes it worse, since the soda drinker pays £26.50 when their turn comes, for £12.50 of drinks. The consumption split solves both: pint drinkers pay £30, the soda drinker pays £12.50, and the total is £132.50.

What non-drinkers can do

  • Say it once, early, lightly. "I'm on water tonight" covers it, and no reason is needed.
  • Offer to be the tally keeper. Being sober makes you well placed to keep track, and it keeps you involved.
  • Join the round in your own way. Buy your own drink, or buy a round for others if you wish. It is your choice.
  • Do not subsidise silently. If you are paying for others' drinks and it bothers you, raise it kindly. A good group will fix it at once.

Splitting a wedding, party or big event with non-drinkers

At big events, the drinks bill can be huge, and non-drinkers can be a large share of the guests. A fair approach is to charge a per-head price for food and a separate drinks package for those who choose one. If a hosted bar is planned, non-drinkers should not be charged for it. If guests are chipping in, make the drinks contribution optional. That avoids the awkward situation where non-drinking guests are asked to fund an open bar they do not use.

The same principle applies to smaller events, such as office leaving drinks. A collection for a gift can be voluntary, while the bar tab is settled individually. The goal is not to hide the cost, but to make the choice visible, so that everybody pays for what they actually take.

How a tracker helps a mixed group

Tracking is especially useful when a group is mixed, because it replaces guesswork with a record. Each round is logged with its cost, and each person's drinks are known. At the end, the difference between what each person paid and what they had is the amount to settle. Non-drinkers do not have to ask for a discount, and drinkers do not have to feel guilty. It is simply what the numbers say.

A short script for the organiser

  1. Before booking: "We'll split food evenly and everyone pays for their own drinks."
  2. At the table: "Drinks are separate. Order what you like."
  3. At the end: "Let's total the food and share it, then each add our own drinks."
  4. For rounds: "We'll track them and settle by what people had."

Four short sentences, said calmly, make the whole thing feel normal. A relaxed organiser sets the tone for everyone else.

A note for hosts and organisers

If you are organising an event, you set the tone. Say early that food is shared and drinks are individual. Offer good non-alcoholic options, and do not price them as an afterthought. Avoid comments about who is or is not drinking. When the bill arrives, use the agreed method without fuss. Guests remember hosts who made the money simple and fair, and they remember the opposite too. A relaxed, inclusive approach makes every guest, drinker or not, feel welcome.

Inclusion beyond the bill

Splitting fairly is only part of being inclusive. Choose venues with good non-alcoholic options, so non-drinkers are not stuck with a single fizzy drink. Avoid pressuring people to join a round or to "have just one". Do not make jokes about who is drinking. Suggest activities beyond the pub now and then, such as dinner, a walk or a game night, so the group is not defined by alcohol. When the group does go out, make sure everyone can take part in the fun, including the spin wheel or the rotation, at whatever level suits them. A group that does these small things keeps its friendships intact, and fair splitting becomes just one more sign of consideration rather than a special arrangement.

How do you split the tip when some friends don't drink?

Continue with the six-person dinner above, where the total came to €194 and the group tips 10%, or €19.40. Sharing that tip in proportion to spending gives each drinker €3.90 and each non-drinker €1.90. Drinkers pay €42.90 in total, non-drinkers €20.90, and the four drinkers plus two non-drinkers add up to €213.40, exactly the bill plus the tip.

An even tip split would have charged everyone €3.23, so the non-drinkers would have paid €1.33 too much each. It is a small sum, but it is the same quiet subsidy again, and proportional sharing removes it. For the wider mechanics, see how to split tax and tip on a bill.

How do you split a bottle of wine when only some of the table drinks?

Only the people who drank from it pay for it. If a €36 bottle is shared by three of six diners, each of the three pays €12 and the other three pay nothing towards it. If someone had a single glass while the others shared the rest, it is fine to agree a rough weighting, but keep it light. Nobody wants to measure glasses.

What if a non-drinker orders the most expensive dish?

Then they pay for it. The point of separating food from drinks is fairness, not a rule that non-drinkers pay less. If food is being split evenly and one person had a steak while the others shared starters, the group can either treat the main courses as personal or accept a slightly uneven food split. Talk about it early. The hybrid method described in how to split a dinner bill with friends handles this well: shared plates are split evenly, and individual mains are paid individually.

What about someone who has just one drink?

Do not build a spreadsheet for a single beer. A light drinker who had one drink should pay for it and share the food, and the group should not renegotiate the method because of it. The goal is to avoid a meaningful subsidy, and a couple of euros either way is not one. If the gap grows across several rounds, that is when tracking what each person had becomes worth it.

What mistakes should you avoid?

  • Waiting for the bill. Suggesting a fair method after the total appears feels like a complaint. Agree it before ordering.
  • Making non-drinkers ask. The fair method should be the default, not a favour.
  • Excluding them from rounds. Include them if they want to be, with whatever they are drinking.
  • Forgetting soft drinks are drinks. Coffee, juice and water with a price count on each person's own tab.

Frequently asked questions

Should non-drinkers pay for alcohol?

No. Non-drinkers should pay for their own food and drinks and an even share of anything genuinely shared, but not for other people's alcohol.

Is an even split ever fine?

Yes, when everyone spent roughly the same. It is also fine if the group agrees beforehand that fairness matters less than simplicity.

How do you split a bill when only some people ordered alcohol?

Split food evenly among everyone, and have each person pay for their own drinks. Share the tip in proportion to what each person spent.

What about a designated driver?

Treat them like any non-drinker: they pay for their own food and soft drinks, and they do not fund rounds of alcohol they did not drink.

Can an app handle mixed groups?

Yes. RoundFair records each round and shows who had what and the cost per person, so the split reflects actual consumption.

Should the group ask why someone is not drinking?

No. The reason is private, and a fair method for splitting the bill works without anyone needing to explain.

What if the non-drinker wants to buy a round?

They can, and it is a generous gesture, but it should be their choice and not an expectation.

Should non-drinkers pay for shared food?

Yes. Food that everyone ate is usually split evenly among all diners, and only the drinks are paid individually.

What if the non-drinker is driving and has one drink?

Let them pay for that drink and share the food. A single drink does not justify changing the method for the whole table.
