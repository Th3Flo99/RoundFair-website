Whose Turn Is It to Buy the Next Round?

2026-09-18 · By FairLead

"Whose turn is it?" is the most argued-over question in any group that drinks together. Buying rounds is a lovely tradition, built on trust and reciprocity, but it depends entirely on everyone remembering who bought what. When memories differ, the tradition quietly turns into resentment.

This guide explains the basic etiquette of round buying, the reasons rounds go unfair, and the practical ways to settle whose turn it is without a debate. If you only want the short version: agree the rules early, keep a running record of what each person has paid, and let the person who is furthest behind go next.

The basic rules of round etiquette

Round etiquette is unwritten, but it is remarkably consistent across pubs and bars. The core principles are:

  • Everyone in the round buys one. If you accept a drink from someone, you are part of the round and you are expected to take your turn.
  • Buy for everyone at the table. Include people who have just arrived, but not people who are clearly leaving.
  • Match the round. If the round is pints, do not order a £14 cocktail without saying so; it changes the cost for the buyer.
  • Do not leave before your turn. Slipping out just before it is your go is the fastest way to lose the group's goodwill.
  • Say so if you are not drinking. Opting out is fine, as long as it is announced early.

Why rounds get unfair

Rounds work when everyone drinks at the same pace and orders similarly priced drinks. Real groups rarely do. Here are the usual reasons fairness breaks down:

Different pace

A fast drinker finishes first and feels pressure to buy sooner. A slow drinker skips rounds because their glass is still half full. Over an evening, the fast drinker spends far more.

Different prices

A group of four, where three drink £5 pints and one drinks £11 cocktails, is not evenly split even if each person buys exactly one round. The cocktail drinker gets a much better deal.

Late arrivals and early exits

Someone joins at round three and someone leaves after round five. Do they owe for rounds they missed? Did they benefit from rounds bought before they arrived? Without a shared rule, each person answers differently.

Bad memory

By round four, nobody is sure who bought round two. Honest people misremember in their own favour, and the group ends up with three people who each believe they bought the last one.

A worked example

Five friends buy four rounds. Round costs: £30, £45, £30, £50. Ben buys rounds one and four (£80). Chloe buys round two (£45). Dan buys round three (£30). Eva and Finn buy nothing because they were "about to".

Total spend is £155, so a fair share is £31 each. Ben is £49 in credit, Chloe £14, and Dan is £1 short of even. Eva and Finn each owe £31. A strict rotation would have felt fair to Ben, Chloe and Dan; only the numbers show who actually owes what. This is why keeping a record of amounts matters more than tracking whose turn it is.

How to handle it fairly

  • Agree the method up front. Rotation, spent-the-least or random, whichever you choose, say it once at the start of the night.
  • Track the total, not just the turns. Amounts reveal what rotation hides.
  • Let the person furthest behind go next. It corrects for uneven rounds and late arrivals.
  • Settle at the end. If someone is still behind at closing time, they send the difference rather than waiting for the next night.
  • Use chance for ties. See six fair ways to decide who buys the next round.

How to remember whose turn it is

The hardest part of round buying is not the money, it is the memory. By the fourth round, five people will give five different answers to "whose go is it?". A few habits make the answer obvious.

Keep one shared record

One notes app, one phone on the table or one app that everyone can see. If the record exists in only one head, it is a matter of opinion; if it is visible, it is a fact. RoundFair's Up now view exists for this exact reason.

Record the round when it is bought, not later

The moment the drinks reach the table is the moment to log the round. Wait ten minutes and the conversation has moved on; wait an hour and you are guessing. It takes a few seconds, and the person who just sat down with the drinks is the natural one to do it.

Say the next name out loud

When the round is logged, say who is up next. "Sam's next." It sounds trivial, but it makes the turn public and gives Sam time to prepare instead of being surprised at the end of a story.

Use physical cues if you must

Some groups pass a beer mat or a coaster: whoever holds it buys the next round, then hands it on. It is charming and it works for small groups, but it does not remember amounts, so it cannot fix uneven rounds.

Rounds with different group sizes and setups

Etiquette changes with the setting. What works for four friends in a pub does not always work in other situations.

  • Work drinks. Rounds are common but people come and go. It is normal to buy for the people currently at the table, and nobody keeps score across weeks. Be generous early and do not fret about small imbalances.
  • A stag or hen weekend. The honoured guest usually does not buy, and the rest share the cost. Agree it in advance. See how to split costs on a bachelor or hen party.
  • A big birthday. The birthday person is often bought drinks rather than buying. Others should still take turns among themselves.
  • A quiet catch-up for two. Take turns, and do not track it. The relationship is the ledger.
  • Mixed drinkers and non-drinkers. Non-drinkers can join the rotation for their own drinks or opt out. See splitting a bill when some friends do not drink.

What good etiquette looks like in practice

Round etiquette is really a bundle of small courtesies. The people who are reliably welcome at a table share a few habits: they go to the bar when it is their turn without being reminded; they buy for the whole group, including the person who has just arrived; they do not order the most expensive item on the menu without saying so; and they notice when someone has been paying more than their share and quietly rebalance it.

The opposite habits are equally recognisable. The person who disappears just before their turn, who arrives late and expects to be bought a drink, or who says "I'll get the next one" and never does. Nobody makes a scene about it, but everyone remembers, and the invitations quietly thin out. If you want a simple test, ask whether the rest of the group would say your turns have been fair. If the honest answer is yes, your etiquette is fine.

Talking about it without spoiling the night

The awkward moment is when someone realises the rounds have been uneven. A light touch is best. Start with the tally rather than the person: "Looks like I've bought three and you've bought one, shall we square up?" is a fact about numbers. "You never buy a round" is an accusation. The first invites a transfer; the second invites a defence.

Choose your moment as well. The end of the night, when the last round is finished, is better than mid-conversation. If the group has agreed a method at the start, you are not raising a grievance; you are just applying the rule. That is why agreeing the method early matters so much: it lets everyone talk about money without it feeling personal. For related guidance, see how to remind friends to pay you back.

A five-minute agreement for your group

The best way to prevent round arguments is to agree the rules once, out loud, and then forget about them. Here is a simple script you can adapt at the start of a night.

  1. "We're buying rounds tonight." Confirms the system.
  2. "Whoever's spent the least goes next, and we'll use the wheel if it's a tie." Gives the method.
  3. "Anyone not drinking says so now." Sorts the exceptions early.
  4. "We'll square up at the end if it's uneven." Settles what happens to imbalances.

That takes under a minute and covers ninety per cent of what goes wrong later. It also sets a friendly tone: everyone has agreed to be fair, so nobody has to police anyone. Groups that do this regularly find they hardly ever have to invoke the rules, because the agreement is enough.

A closing word on round etiquette

Round etiquette is about generosity and trust. It works because people are willing to give a little more than they strictly need to, and to assume that others will do the same. When that trust is tested, a simple tally is not a sign of distrust but of care: it lets the group be generous without anyone being quietly out of pocket. Keep the tradition alive by taking your turn, being clear about what you are doing and settling up when the numbers say you should. The best round-buying groups are those in which nobody thinks about money at all, and a fair system in the background is what makes that possible.

How do you work out whose turn it is when rounds are uneven?

Skip the mental rotation and look at money. Write down each person's running total, then send the person with the lowest figure to the bar. Suppose four friends have had three rounds costing £24, £36 and £20. Mia bought the first (£24), Tom the second (£36) and Lena the third (£20), while Raj has bought nothing because he arrived at round three. The totals are Tom £36, Mia £24, Lena £20, Raj £0. Raj is up, and he stays up until his total passes the others. A plain rotation would have sent Mia next, even though she has already paid more than Lena and far more than Raj.

What are the most common round buying mistakes?

  • Everyone assumes someone else is counting. In a group of six, five people believe the sixth is keeping track. Nobody is. Name a tracker or use an app.
  • Counting rounds instead of pounds. One round of doubles and one of halves are not the same debt.
  • Vanishing at your turn. Toilet trips and phone calls that happen to coincide with your go are noticed. Just go.
  • Buying out of turn to look generous. It feels kind, but it wrecks the tally and makes others feel they owe you.
  • Raising it too late. A comment at round two is friendly. The same comment at closing time is an accusation.

What is the difference between buying rounds and splitting the bill?

Rounds are pay-as-you-go: the total cost is spread out over the evening by turns, and nobody hands over money at the end. Splitting is pay-at-the-end: one bill, divided by what each person consumed. Rounds suit small, stable groups who drink similarly. Splitting suits big, mixed or one-off groups. Many groups do a hybrid, buying rounds for most of the night and settling any imbalance at closing. How should a group agree the rules at the start of the night?

Thirty seconds at the first round saves an argument at the fifth. Cover four points out loud.

  1. Method. Rotation, spent-the-least or random. If in doubt, pick spent-the-least and use a wheel for ties.
  2. Who is in. Anyone not drinking says so now. Anyone arriving late joins the tally at zero.
  3. Drink limits. Agree if a round means "the usual" or if cocktails and spirits are fair game.
  4. Settling. Decide whether gaps get paid back at the end or carried to next time.

What do you say when someone is not pulling their weight?

Keep it light and factual. "I think it's your go, I've done the last two" works better than a speech. If it keeps happening, show the running totals instead of describing the pattern. Numbers take the sting out of it because you are pointing at a list, not at a person. And if you are the one who always drifts off, take the hint and get up first next time.

Etiquette for awkward situations

  • You only had one drink. Say so at the start and pay for your own. Nobody minds an honest opt-out.
  • You are short of cash. Say it before the round starts, not when it is your turn. A friend will usually cover you, and you can settle later.
  • The group grows mid-evening. Decide whether newcomers join the current tally or start a fresh one. Either is fine if it is stated.
  • Someone always skips. Raise it kindly and lightly ("I think it's your turn, mate") rather than letting the resentment build. A visible tally makes this conversation easier because it is about numbers, not character.

Frequently asked questions

Is it rude not to buy a round?

Yes, if you accept drinks from the group. Either take your turn or say early that you are only paying for your own drinks, and most groups will happily agree.

What if I only had one drink?

Tell the group at the start and pay for your own. Joining the round for a single drink is unfair to the buyers, and most people will understand if you opt out openly.

How do you keep track of rounds?

Note each round's cost and buyer as it happens. RoundFair does this for you and shows whose turn it is and who is behind, so nobody has to remember.

Does the round have to be the same drink for everyone?

No, but the buyer should know what they are committing to. If drinks vary a lot in price, track amounts so the totals stay fair.

What should we do if someone leaves early?

Settle what they owe or are owed before they go, or note it in your tally so it can be sorted the next day.

How do you remember whose turn it is?

Keep one shared record, log each round when it is bought, and say the next name aloud. An app such as RoundFair shows whose turn it is at a glance.

Is it okay to keep track of rounds openly?

Yes, if the group agrees. A visible tally is fairer than private scorekeeping, because it takes the argument out of memory.

Who should buy the next round if I arrive late?

You join the tally at zero, so you are usually the person furthest behind and can go up early. You do not owe for rounds you missed unless the group agrees otherwise.

Is it OK to buy out of turn?

Occasionally, as a gift, if you say so out loud. Otherwise it breaks the tally and makes others feel they owe you, so keep to the agreed order.
