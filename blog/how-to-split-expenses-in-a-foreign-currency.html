How to Split Expenses in a Foreign Currency

2026-09-19 · By FairLead

Pick one base currency for the whole group, convert every expense at a single agreed rate, and settle in that currency. This avoids arguments and hidden card fees.

Splitting costs is hard enough at home. Abroad, every expense arrives in a currency nobody thinks in, cards add fees, and each person's bank converts at a different rate. The result is a stack of small unfairnesses that turn into arguments on the flight home. This guide gives a five-step method, compares the options for choosing a rate, shows how to avoid card fees, and covers how nights out fit in.

Step by step

  1. Choose a base currency, usually the one most of the group uses at home.
  2. Agree a rate. Use the mid-market rate from a reputable source, or the rate your card actually charged.
  3. Convert each expense into the base currency when you record it.
  4. Total and split using your chosen method.
  5. Settle in one transfer per person.

Which rate should you use?

Mid-market rate on the dayNeutral and easy to checkNot what your card charged
Your card's rateExact cost to youDiffers per person
A fixed rate for the tripSimple, no daily updatesDrifts on long trips

For most groups, the mid-market rate on the day of settling, or a single fixed rate agreed before the trip, is the least contentious. What matters is that everyone accepted it before any money moved.

A worked example

Four friends from the euro area spend a week in a country with a different currency. The payer, Sam, covers a rental car for 240 local units and groceries for 160 units. Alex pays a dinner for 200 units. The agreed rate is 0.90 euro per local unit.

  • Sam: 400 units × 0.90 = €360
  • Alex: 200 units × 0.90 = €180
  • Total: €540, so €135 each

Sam is owed €360 − €135 = €225; Alex is owed €180 − €135 = €45; Jo and Priya each owe €135. Jo sends Sam €135, Priya sends Sam €90 and Alex €45. Three transfers settle the trip, all in euros.

Avoid card fees

  • Pay in local currency, not your home currency, when a terminal offers a choice.
  • Use a card with no foreign transaction fee.
  • Withdraw larger amounts less often.
  • Agree who covers a fee if one person pays for the group on a fee-charging card.

The terminal that offers to charge your home currency almost always gives a worse rate than your bank. Decline it and pay in local currency.

Cash versus card

If you pay in cash, keep a note of each expense in the moment, because cash leaves no receipt trail on your statement. A shared note or a single person acting as the "wallet" keeps the record clean. Card payments are easier to reconstruct later, but each person's statement will show a different converted amount, so rely on the local-currency amount on the receipt rather than the statement.

A pre-trip checklist

  • Choose the base currency and the rate rule in the group chat before you leave.
  • Decide who records expenses, or use one shared note.
  • Check which cards have foreign transaction fees and who will use them.
  • Agree the settle-up date, for example the Sunday after you return.
  • Keep a photo of any large receipt in local currency.

Ten minutes of agreement before the flight prevents nearly every currency argument. Most disputes are not about the rate itself, but about a rate nobody chose in advance. If a friend leaves the trip early, settle their share at the same rate before they go, so it does not linger.

Recording expenses abroad: a simple system

A good record-keeping habit is what makes multi-currency splitting painless. For each shared expense, note four things: the date, the amount in local currency, who paid and who it covers. Add the converted amount in your base currency when you have a rate. A shared note, a spreadsheet or a ledger app will do. The key is to record at the time, not at the end.

Take a photo of any large receipt. If you pay by card, the statement will eventually show the converted amount, but each person's bank will convert differently, so use the local-currency amount on the receipt as the source of truth and convert at your agreed rate.

Splitting a trip that crosses several countries

If the trip visits more than one country, you have more than one local currency. Choose a single base currency for the whole group and record every expense in it, converted at an agreed rate for each country. Agree a rate per country at the start, or use a mid-market rate at the time of settling. Do not mix methods, since that creates small discrepancies that grow into arguments.

Keep cash and card records separate if it helps. Cash withdrawals in local currency are a shared cost only if the cash is spent on shared items, so track what the cash was used for. A shared kitty of local cash, topped up by everyone, is a simple way to handle small daily expenses.

Common mistakes when splitting in another currency

  • Converting at different rates. Pick one rate per currency and stick to it.
  • Ignoring card fees. A small percentage on every payment adds up. Agree who bears it.
  • Accepting the terminal's home-currency offer. It usually gives a worse rate.
  • Mixing up decimal separators. Some countries use a comma where you expect a point. Check before entering a figure.
  • Waiting too long to settle. Rates move, and memories fade.

Nights out abroad

Evenings are where currency gets confusing, because drinks are bought quickly and in small amounts. The simplest approach is to treat each night as its own tally, in local currency, and settle at the end with a single conversion. Taking turns to buy rounds avoids converting every drink, since each person pays whatever the round costs in the moment. If the rounds were uneven, compare totals in local currency, convert the difference once and settle. Understanding exchange rates

An exchange rate is the price of one currency in another. The mid-market rate is the midpoint between what buyers and sellers are prepared to pay, and it is the fair benchmark. Banks, card networks and exchange bureaus add a margin or a fee, so the rate you actually receive is usually a little worse. That is not a scam, just how the business works, but it means that "the rate" is not one number. When you agree a rate as a group, you are choosing which number to treat as the truth.

For most trips, the difference between the mid-market rate and a card rate is small, one or two per cent. The bigger risk is a bad rate from a dynamic conversion prompt or an airport bureau, which can cost several per cent. Avoid those, and the choice of benchmark matters less than agreeing on one.

Splitting when cards have different fees

If some friends have no-fee cards and others have fee-charging cards, fairness gets awkward. The easiest solution is to ask the no-fee card holders to make group payments. If that is not possible, decide how fees are shared. Either the payer absorbs their own fee, since it is their card, or the group shares it as part of the total. The first is simpler, and the second is fairer if one person's card is used for convenience. Tell everyone the rule at the start.

Cash withdrawals add another layer. ATM fees, both from the bank and the machine, are a cost of paying with cash. Withdraw larger amounts less often, and treat the fee as a shared cost if the cash was used for the group.

Settling up after the trip

At the end, convert every expense into the base currency at the agreed rate, and sum them. Each person's fair share is the total divided by the number of people, adjusted for any unequal shares. Subtract what each person paid, and the result is what they owe or are owed. Then settle with the smallest number of transfers, in the base currency.

Choose a settlement method that works across borders. If everyone banks in the same currency zone, a direct bank transfer is fine. If not, a widely available payment service may be simpler. Send the money within a week, and share confirmation in the group chat. A clean, prompt end to a trip leaves everyone with good memories and no lingering awkwardness.

Tips for keeping things simple

  • Choose one person to record group expenses, and let them focus on it.
  • Photograph receipts for anything above a threshold, such as €50.
  • Use a shared kitty of local cash for small daily costs.
  • Do not agonise over cents. Round to the nearest whole unit.
  • Take turns buying rounds on nights out, so you avoid converting every drink.

These habits reduce the work and the arguments. The goal is a fair result and a relaxed trip, not a perfect ledger.

A worked example with three currencies

A group of four travels through two countries with different currencies and returns home. Costs are recorded in local currency, then converted to the home currency at fixed rates agreed in advance: 0.90 for the first country and 1.10 for the second. Sam pays 500 units in the first country (€450) and 200 units in the second (€220). Alex pays 300 units in the second (€330). Total: €1,000. Fair share: €250 each. Sam paid €670 and is owed €420. Alex paid €330 and is owed €80. Jo and Priya each owe €250. Jo sends Sam €250, Priya sends Sam €170 and Alex €80. Three transfers, one currency, no arguments about rates.

Why fixed rates reduce arguments

Fixed rates remove a whole category of dispute. Nobody can claim that a rate on a particular day favoured someone. Everyone knows the numbers in advance. The trade-off is that a fixed rate may drift from the real market over a long trip, but for most holidays the difference is small. If the trip is long or the currency is volatile, agree to update the rate at a set point, such as halfway, and apply the new rate only to later expenses. Either way, write the rates down in the group chat, and keep to them.

A note on cash, cards and mobile wallets abroad

The way you pay abroad affects how you record costs. Cash is simple but leaves no trail, so note each shared expense. Cards give a statement but at a converted amount, so use the local-currency figure on the receipt. Mobile wallets behave like cards but may show a different converted figure, so treat them the same. If a group member pays for everything on one card, their statement becomes a useful summary, but check it against the receipts to be sure. A blend of methods is normal on a trip, so the safest habit is to record each shared expense at the time, in the local currency, and convert at your agreed rate at the end.

What if the group has two different home currencies?

Mixed groups are common: two friends from the UK, two from the euro area, all in a third country. Pick the base currency that most of the group already thinks in, or the one the biggest payer used, and convert everything into it. Do not convert half into pounds and half into euros. Two bases mean two rates, and every additional rate is another chance for a disagreement.

If someone would rather settle in their own currency, let them convert the final amount they owe at the agreed rate on the day they send it. The trip total stays in one currency, and each person handles their own last step. Just remember that the agreed rate applies to the trip's costs, while the bank's rate applies to the transfer, so small differences at this stage are normal and not worth arguing about.

How do I convert each expense, step by step?

Suppose the base currency is euros and the agreed rate is 1 local unit = €0.90. Keep a simple log with four columns: what, who paid, amount in local currency, amount in euros.

WhatPaid byLocal unitsEuros at 0.90
Taxi from the airportJo60€54.00
GroceriesSam85€76.50
Museum ticketsPriya120€108.00
Beach lunchAlex95€85.50

The total is €324. Split four ways, each person's fair share is €81. Jo paid €54, so Jo owes €27. Sam paid €76.50 and owes €4.50. Priya paid €108 and is owed €27. Alex paid €85.50 and is owed €4.50. Sam and Jo pay Priya and Alex, and everything balances to zero. Round each conversion to the cent, not to the euro, because rounding errors add up over a long trip.

Should the payer or the group cover the card fee?

This is the question people rarely raise until the statement arrives. Suppose Jo's card adds a 2% fee on every foreign transaction (check your own bank, because fees vary a lot). On €324 of spending, that would be about €6.50 in total. Three sensible options exist.

  • Payer absorbs it. Simplest, and fair if the payer chose the card. It suits small amounts.
  • Add the fee to the shared total. Each person pays a little more. Fair when the group asked one person to pay for everything.
  • Use the fee-free card. The best answer is to pay with the card that has no fee, and alternate who fronts the big costs.

Whichever you pick, say it before the trip. A fee added at the end feels like a surprise; a fee agreed at the start is just part of the plan.

What are the common mistakes when splitting across currencies?

  • Converting the same amount twice. If a receipt shows both currencies, use the local one. The other may include the terminal's own margin.
  • Using each person's statement. The figures differ per bank and per day. Use the receipt and your agreed rate.
  • Mixing rates. Convert some expenses at the day's rate and some at a trip rate, and the numbers will never reconcile.
  • Forgetting cash. Cash spent from a shared pot must be logged too. If the pot came from a withdrawal, count the withdrawal as the expense and do not count each purchase again.
  • Settling in the local currency. Sending local currency across borders means a second conversion and a second fee. Settle in the base currency.

How do nights out fit in when everything is in a foreign currency?

Bars are where currency maths gets painful, because you have many small purchases in local money. The easiest solution is to avoid converting them at all. When friends take turns buying whole rounds, each round is one purchase and the totals roughly even out over the evening. Nobody needs to convert a single drink; the only number that matters is who owes the difference, if anyone does.

Frequently asked questions

Which exchange rate should we use?

Any rate the whole group agrees on in advance. The mid-market rate is the fairest neutral default.

Should we settle in the local currency?

Usually no. Settle in the base currency to avoid converting twice.

Does RoundFair convert currencies?

No. RoundFair focuses on rounds and bill splitting, so use a ledger app for multi-currency trips.

Who pays the card fee?

Agree it in advance. Either the payer absorbs it, or it is added to the shared total and split.

What if the rate changes during the trip?

Use one fixed rate for the whole trip, or the rate on the settle-up day, and apply it to everything.

Should we convert every expense or only the big ones?

Convert every expense at the same agreed rate. Mixing converted and unconverted amounts makes the totals impossible to reconcile.

What if the group has more than one home currency?

Pick one base currency for the trip total. Anyone who prefers their own currency can convert the final amount they owe at the agreed rate.
