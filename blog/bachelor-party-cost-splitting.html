How to Split Costs on a Bachelor or Hen Party

2026-09-12 · By Florian Ronsse

A bachelor or hen party is a weekend of shared costs: accommodation, activities, food and a lot of drinks. It is also a classic setting for money awkwardness, because the group is often mixed in budget, the guest of honour is not expected to pay, and the drinking makes tracking harder.

This guide explains how to split costs on a bachelor or hen party fairly: setting a realistic budget, deciding who covers the guest of honour, collecting money early, separating shared and optional costs, keeping a running record, and handling the drinking.

Start with a realistic budget

Ask the group early what they can spend, and be honest about it. A weekend plan that suits the organiser's budget can exclude half the guests. A useful approach is to set a target price per person, then plan the weekend to fit it, rather than the other way round.

Give a range instead of one number ("€250–€350 for the weekend, excluding travel"), and make it clear that opting out of individual activities is fine. That way, guests can choose their own level without feeling excluded.

Decide who pays for the guest of honour

The norm is that the guest of honour does not pay for their own weekend, and the cost is shared by the others. Agree the arithmetic openly:

  • Guests split the honouree's share evenly. With ten people and nine paying, each pays 1/9 more.
  • Include this in the price you quote. "€300 each" should already contain the honouree's share, so nobody is surprised later.
  • Agree partial cover if needed. Some honourees prefer to pay for part, for example their own travel.

The same principle applies to dinners; see how to split a dinner bill with friends.

Collect money early

The organiser should not be fronting thousands out of their own pocket. Collect a deposit as soon as the plan is confirmed, and the balance before the weekend.

  • Use a bank transfer or payment app so there is a record.
  • Set clear deadlines, and stick to them.
  • Collect for accommodation and activities first, since those are booked in advance.
  • Keep a separate pot, or a separate account, for the group's money.

Keeping the optional list separate means someone who skips the go-karting does not pay for it. It keeps costs down for people on a tighter budget, and it stops resentment before it builds.

Keep a running record

Record every shared expense as it happens: amount, who paid, and who it covers. A shared note or expense app works fine. At the end of the weekend, compare what each person paid with their fair share and settle in a few transfers. The same five-step system applies as for any group trip; see splitting group trip costs without falling out.

Handle the drinking

The drinks are where the weekend gets uneven. Some guests drink heavily, some barely at all, and rounds are bought at speed. A few workable approaches:

  • Pot for shared drinks. Each guest adds a set amount, and shared bottles and rounds come from the pot. Simple, but non-drinkers pay the same.
  • Rounds with tracking. Buy rounds as usual, log what each costs, and settle by consumption at the end. See round buying vs splitting the bill.
  • Individual tabs. Best for a big group where drinking habits differ widely.

The honouree should not be paying for rounds, so exclude their share from the tally.

Running the weekend without awkwardness

  • Keep the honouree out of the money. They should not be chasing payments or reading spreadsheets.
  • Name one organiser for money. Two people managing it leads to double-charging and confusion.
  • Share the plan early. A written itinerary with prices lets guests opt in or out knowingly.
  • Settle within a week. Debts after a big weekend are easy to forget, so do the final transfers quickly.

For the drinking side, see how to run a bar tab with friends.

How to talk about money with the group

The most awkward part of organising is the first message about cost. Keep it factual and friendly: state the dates, the plan, the price range and the payment deadline. Invite people to say if it is beyond their budget, privately if they prefer. Avoid language that pressures, such as "everyone's in", until people have had time to think.

Give a simple breakdown: this much for accommodation, this much for activities, this much for food. Transparency makes people feel respected, and it heads off the suspicion that the organiser is making a profit or quietly padding. Send a reminder a week before each deadline, and thank people when they pay.

Splitting costs when the group is mixed

Bachelor and hen groups are often a mix of close friends and acquaintances, with very different budgets. A few practices keep it inclusive.

  • Offer tiers. A basic package covers the core weekend, with add-ons for those who want more.
  • Allow partial attendance. Someone who can only come for one night should pay only for that night.
  • Share costs early. Nobody should discover the price after they have committed.
  • Keep the drinks fair. Rounds are fun, but they are where the costs diverge most, so track them separately.

Nights out: keeping the rounds fair

The nights out are the heart of the weekend and the hardest part to keep fair. Drinks flow quickly, rounds are bought in bursts and the honoured guest is usually not paying. Two things help. First, agree at the start whether the group is running a shared pot, individual tabs or tracked rounds. Second, keep a record of each round and its cost.

With tracked rounds, the settlement at the end is easy. Each person's consumption is compared with what they paid, and the difference is transferred. The honoured guest's drinks are treated as a group cost and split among the rest, so they never see the bill. Common mistakes when splitting costs for a stag or hen

  • Not telling guests the total. Surprises about cost spoil a weekend.
  • Forgetting the honouree's share. The others cover it, so include it in the price.
  • Not collecting deposits. The organiser ends up fronting large sums.
  • Booking non-refundable items too early. If someone drops out, the group is left with the cost.
  • Leaving the drinks untracked. That is where resentment builds.
  • Making it too expensive. The best plans respect the tightest budget.

Talking to guests who cannot afford it

Some guests will decline quietly because of cost. Make it easy for them to speak to you. Offer a reduced version of the weekend, such as joining for one night, and keep the conversation private. Never comment on someone's finances in the group chat. If the organiser knows a friend is struggling, it is kind to plan a cheaper option from the start rather than creating a situation where someone has to ask. A weekend everyone can afford is a better weekend for everyone.

The honouree's role in the money

The honouree should not have to worry about money, but a good one will still be thoughtful. Being clear about their preferences, such as budget limits or activities to avoid, helps the organiser plan. Offering to cover part of the cost is generous but not expected. Thanking the group at the end, sincerely and perhaps with a round of drinks on the last night, is the right gesture. On the last evening, RoundFair can keep track of who bought what, so even the honouree's thank-you round is fairly recorded.

After the weekend: closing the books

Within a week of the weekend, share a final summary: what was collected, what was spent and any balance. If there is money left in the pot, return it in equal shares or use it for a group meal. If someone is owed, pay them promptly. Publishing the numbers builds trust, and it makes the organiser's work visible. It also closes the matter, so nobody is left wondering whether the accounts were settled.

A short template for the first message

"Hi all! We're planning a weekend for [name] on [dates] in [place]. Estimated cost is €[range] per person, which covers accommodation, the group dinner, one activity and transport. Drinks are separate or tracked. Please reply by [date] to confirm, and let me know privately if the budget is a stretch, because I can adjust. I'll collect a deposit of €[amount] by [date] by bank transfer. Thanks!" That covers the essentials, makes it easy to opt out gracefully and sets a clear deadline.

Splitting costs when guests travel from different places

Guests arriving from different cities face very different travel costs. Keep travel personal: each person pays for their own journey, and the shared budget covers only what happens once everyone has arrived. If the group meets at a station or airport, shared transfers are split among those who use them. Be understanding about arrival and departure times, since someone flying in late may miss the first dinner and should not pay for it. State this in the first message, so nobody has to ask. Handling these details early keeps the weekend about the celebration, not the invoices, and it stops the quiet resentment that builds when someone feels they subsidised other people's plans.

How do you turn the total budget into a price per guest?

Start from the sample budget of €2,600 for nine paying guests, which is €288.89 each. Add a 10% buffer for surprises and the total becomes €2,860, or €317.78 each. Round up to €320 per guest, which raises €2,880 and leaves a small cushion. Collect 40% as a deposit, €128, and the remaining €192 before the weekend. If the buffer is not used, refund it evenly or spend it on a group dinner on the final night.

What if a guest drops out after paying a deposit?

State the policy when you first share the plan. A fair rule is that deposits cover costs already committed, such as accommodation booked, and are only refunded if the space can be filled or the booking is refundable. Everything not yet booked is returned. If the group has to absorb a lost share, spread it across the remaining guests with plenty of notice rather than surprising them on the weekend.

What about partners and guests who join for only part of the weekend?

Price them by what they use. Someone who arrives for Saturday's dinner pays for the dinner and the night, not for Friday's activities. Use a per-night approach for the accommodation, as described in splitting costs on a group trip, and a fixed price for each activity so people can opt in or out.

How do you keep the drinking fair for guests who drink less?

A shared drinks pot only works when everyone drinks about the same. If some guests barely drink, offer a pot with a small contribution and track everything above it individually. See splitting a bill when some friends do not drink for a fair way to separate food from drinks, and make the split the default rather than something non-drinkers have to request.

How do you chase late payments politely?

Send one reminder with the amount, the deadline and the payment details, and keep the tone light. If it is still outstanding, message the person privately, never in the group chat. The organiser should not have to make it awkward, so a short template helps: "Hi, just a reminder that the balance of €192 is due by Friday. Here are the details." More wording ideas are in how to remind friends to pay you back, and payment apps are covered in how to split and charge friends on Venmo.

What are the most common money mistakes?

  • Quoting a price without the honouree's share. The final cost jumps at the last minute.
  • Paying everything from the organiser's own account. One missed transfer becomes a large personal loss.
  • No buffer. Taxis, tips and a forgotten deposit eat the budget.
  • Hiding the itinerary. Guests cannot opt out of what they never knew about.

A sample budget

For a ten-person weekend where nine guests share the honouree's costs: accommodation €900, activities €450, group dinners €600, transport €250, shared drinks pot €400. Total: €2,600, which is about €289 per paying guest. Add a small buffer of 5–10% for surprises, and you have a price to quote.

Frequently asked questions

Who pays for the bachelor or hen party?

Traditionally the guests share the cost, including the guest of honour's portion. Agree this openly before booking.

How much should a bachelor party cost per person?

It depends on the destination and length, so start with the group's budget and plan to fit. A range with optional extras is fairer than a single high price.

What if someone cannot afford it?

Offer a cheaper option, such as fewer optional activities, and keep the conversation private. Nobody should feel forced to overspend.

How do you split drinks on a bachelor party?

Either use a shared pot, run individual tabs, or buy rounds and track them so everyone settles by consumption at the end.

When should the money be collected?

Collect a deposit as soon as the plan is fixed, and the balance before the weekend, so the organiser is never out of pocket.

Should the honouree pay for any of the weekend?

Traditionally not, but some prefer to cover part, such as their own travel. Agree it openly.

How do we handle people dropping out?

Set a payment deadline and a clear policy for refunds, especially for non-refundable bookings. Say it in the first message.

How much should each guest pay for a bachelor or hen party?

Divide the total cost, including the honouree's share and a 5-10% buffer, by the number of paying guests, and quote a round figure.

What happens to leftover money after the weekend?

Refund it evenly, or agree beforehand to spend it on a group dinner or a gift for the honouree.
